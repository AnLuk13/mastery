# 7. Fine-Tuning & Customization

Goal: know what fine-tuning actually is mechanically, and — more importantly — recognize the (fairly narrow) set of situations where it's actually the right tool, since chapter 6 already made the case that it's usually not the first thing to reach for.

## 7.1 What fine-tuning actually is

Recall chapter 3 §3.3: a released model has already been through pretraining, instruction tuning, and RLHF. **Fine-tuning** is a further, much smaller training run *on top of* that already-trained model, using your own dataset of examples, to shift its behavior toward your specific task — mechanically the same gradient-descent loop from chapter 2 §2.4, just starting from already-good weights instead of random ones, and using a dataset that's typically hundreds to low-thousands of examples rather than the trillions of tokens pretraining used.

```
Base model (already pretrained + instruction-tuned)
        │
        │  further training on YOUR (input, ideal-output) examples
        ▼
Fine-tuned model — same architecture, adjusted weights
```

This is a genuinely different scale of effort than anything else in this guide so far: it requires assembling a real training dataset, running (or paying a provider to run) an actual training job, and then hosting/serving the resulting custom model — versus a RAG pipeline or a better prompt, which you can iterate on in minutes.

## 7.2 What fine-tuning is actually good at

Fine-tuning shines when the problem is about **behavior, format, or style** — not about giving the model access to facts it lacks (that's RAG, chapter 6 §6.5):

- **Consistent output format/style** at a level few-shot prompting (chapter 4 §4.2) can't reliably hold across thousands of varied inputs — e.g. always producing a specific structured report format for hundreds of different document types.
- **Domain-specific "voice"** — matching a very particular tone or terminology consistently, more reliably than repeatedly re-explaining it in every prompt.
- **Teaching a smaller, cheaper model to imitate a larger one on a narrow task** — fine-tune a small open-weight model (chapter 15 §15.2) on examples generated by a larger, more expensive model for one specific narrow job, then serve the small model in production at a fraction of the cost/latency (a real, common pattern called **distillation**).
- **Reducing prompt length/cost at scale** — if you're currently spending hundreds of tokens per call re-explaining the same formatting rules, baking that behavior into weights via fine-tuning can shrink the per-call prompt (and therefore cost) dramatically at high volume.

## 7.3 What fine-tuning is bad at (the mistake to avoid)

**Teaching the model new facts.** This is the single most common fine-tuning mistake: fine-tuning on a company's internal documents, hoping the model "learns the content," when what actually happens is the model partially memorizes fragments of the training examples (unreliably — see overfitting, chapter 2 §2.5) while gaining no ability to look anything up, cite a source, or stay current when the underlying documents change. RAG solves the "give the model facts" problem far better, more cheaply, more updatably, and more verifiably. If your actual goal is "the model should be able to answer questions about our documents," fine-tuning is very likely the wrong tool even though it sounds intuitively right.

## 7.4 LoRA / PEFT — how fine-tuning got cheap enough to be practical

Fully fine-tuning a large model — updating *every one* of its billions of parameters — is nearly as expensive as pretraining, and out of reach for almost anyone outside a well-funded lab. **PEFT** (Parameter-Efficient Fine-Tuning) techniques, most notably **LoRA** (Low-Rank Adaptation), solve this by freezing the entire original model and training only a small number of *additional*, much smaller weight matrices injected alongside the existing layers — often well under 1% of the original parameter count. At inference time, the small LoRA weights are combined with (or swapped in alongside) the frozen base model. This is *why* fine-tuning became broadly accessible in recent years — a LoRA fine-tuning job can run on a single consumer-grade GPU in hours, versus the datacenter-scale infrastructure full fine-tuning requires, and why Ollama (chapter 13) and most open-model tooling can load a small LoRA "adapter" file on top of a base model you already have.

You will almost never need to implement LoRA math yourself — but recognizing the name, and understanding *why* it exists (make fine-tuning cheap by only training a small add-on instead of the whole model), matters for reading any real fine-tuning documentation, which assumes you know this.

## 7.5 A practical decision checklist

Before fine-tuning anything, work through this in order — each "no" usually means you don't need fine-tuning yet:

1. Is the problem "the model doesn't know X" (facts, current data, private documents)? → **Use RAG (chapter 6), not fine-tuning.**
2. Is the problem "the model doesn't behave/format consistently enough"? → **Try a better system prompt + few-shot examples (chapter 4) first** — it's free to iterate and often sufficient.
3. Have you genuinely exhausted prompting and RAG, and the remaining gap is specifically about consistent behavior/style/format at scale? → **Fine-tuning is now a reasonable option**, with a real (if PEFT-reduced) cost in data, tooling, and ongoing maintenance (a fine-tuned model needs to be re-tuned again if the base model updates or requirements shift).
